Pets & Kids

Planning for family and pet use

There is no blanket safety verdict for every artificial-turf product or every animal. Suitability depends on the exact fibres, backing, infill, drainage, surface heat, cleaning products, and your pet's behaviour.

Ask for current documentation for every proposed component, prevent chewing or ingestion, remove waste promptly, and provide shade and fresh water in hot weather. Ask your veterinarian about animal-specific health concerns. Our pet and child planning guide explains the practical checks.

Use a routine matched to the complete installed system:

  • Solid waste: Remove it promptly, then clean any residue as the product instructions allow.
  • Urine and rinse water: Rinse only where the base, soil, and outlet can carry the water safely.
  • Cleaner: Use only a pet-appropriate product approved for the exact turf, infill, backing, and adhesive. Never mix cleaners.

Frequency depends on use, heat, shade, airflow, and drainage. Persistent odour can indicate contaminated infill or base, or a drainage problem; stronger chemicals may not solve it. See the maintenance and cleaning guide.

Residential landscape turf is not automatically playground safety surfacing. Suitability depends on the complete system, intended activity, surface heat, chemical documentation, accessibility needs, and supervision.

Under elevated play equipment, require a complete protective surfacing system with impact performance appropriate to the fall height; ordinary turf carpet alone is not evidence of fall protection. Licensed Ontario child-care playgrounds have specific CSA Z614 requirements. For a home play area, review the proposed design with a qualified playground professional when fall protection is needed.

No surface can guarantee that a determined dog will not dig. Turf may remove exposed soil from the centre of an area, but dogs can still pull at loose edges, seams, transitions, or existing damaged spots.

Tell the installer about your dog's behaviour before design. Secure the perimeter and transitions for the actual site, then inspect them regularly and repair lifting before it becomes a chewing, digging, or trip hazard.

General Questions

Basic information about artificial turf

There is no single standard recipe. A system can include polyethylene, polypropylene, or nylon fibres; primary and secondary backings; coatings or adhesives; drainage openings or a permeable backing; optional infill or a shock pad; seam tape, fasteners, and edging; plus the prepared base.

Composition varies by product, and a component test does not describe the complete installed system. Ask for the manufacturer, exact SKU, fibre and backing composition, infill or pad, product data sheet, and current test documentation. See how artificial turf is made.

Appearance varies by product, pile height, blade shape, colour blend, infill, lighting, and installation.

Review physical samples and completed-project photos, and compare them in the conditions where the turf will be used.

Artificial turf can become substantially hotter than living grass in direct sun. The actual surface temperature varies with the product, infill, colour, shade, air temperature, wind, and time of day; there is no universal safe air-temperature cutoff.

Check the real surface before barefoot, child, or pet use. Provide shade, footwear, drinking water, and cooler play times. Water may cool the surface only temporarily. See the GTA heat-planning guide.

There is no honest blanket “eco-friendly” verdict. Artificial turf can reduce routine irrigation, mowing, and some lawn treatments, but cleaning or temporary cooling can still use water. It also has manufacturing, heat, runoff, plastic-wear, habitat, replacement, and end-of-life impacts.

Compare it with what it would replace—such as an irrigated lawn, native planting, permeable paving, or a mixed yard—and include the complete lifecycle and local drainage plan. See the environmental lifecycle guide.

Cost & Pricing

Understanding the investment

Installed cost is property-specific. Important factors include:

  • Access and waste removal
  • Excavation, grading, and drainage
  • Edges, shapes, and adjoining surfaces
  • Turf, infill, and other selected materials
  • Project size and site conditions

We review the site details and provide an itemized proposal.

A project-specific proposal should identify, as applicable:

  • Measured area, access, and site assumptions
  • Removal, disposal, excavation, and site preparation
  • Base, grading, drainage, edging, and exact turf and infill SKUs
  • Labour, cleanup, taxes, options, and exclusions
  • Change conditions and applicable product and workmanship documents

Review the written scope before accepting it. Any requested change or newly identified condition should be discussed and documented before additional work proceeds.

Ontario's home-renovation guidance says contracts worth more than $50 must be in writing. When an estimate forms part of the contract, the final price generally cannot exceed it by more than 10% unless the customer agrees to new work or a new price and signs a contract change.

Value depends on your current lawn-care spending, water use, project cost, intended ownership period, ongoing turf care, and personal priorities.

Compare your own documented costs and expected use rather than relying on a guaranteed payback period, savings amount, or property-value increase.

Installation

What to expect during the process

There is no reliable size-only installation timeline. Access, removal, excavation, subgrade, grading, drainage, utility locates or approvals, weather, material availability, crew plan, edges, and adjoining hardscape can all change the schedule.

After the site and scope are reviewed, the written proposal should give a project-specific estimate and identify its assumptions and major dependencies. A schedule estimate is not a guarantee against weather or unforeseen site conditions.

When a living lawn is being replaced, vegetation and unsuitable organic material are typically removed from the work area. The proposal should state what is removed or retained, how material is handled, and any limits around tree roots, utilities, or adjoining features.

Excavation depth and base materials must be designed for the soil, finished grades, drainage path, intended loads, and selected system. A universal depth or generic rock-and-sand layer does not by itself guarantee stability or drainage.

Sometimes. The existing surface must first be checked for stability, condition, slope, drainage outlets, finished height, trip points, and safe transitions. An underlay may be useful for a specific design, but it does not correct trapped water or automatically create playground fall protection.

Balconies and rooftops also require review of waterproofing, structural loads, drainage, fire and building requirements, and any condominium or property-manager rules before work begins. The approved attachment method must not compromise a roof or balcony membrane.

Whether you need to be home is project-specific. Agree on site access, working hours, security, pet safety, utilities, decision authority, and change approvals before work starts. The written pre-start plan should state when an owner or authorized representative must be available.

Useful checkpoints normally include:

  • The pre-start scope and access walkthrough
  • Approval of any documented change
  • The final inspection and care handoff

Maintenance

Keeping your turf looking great

Artificial turf needs different maintenance, not zero maintenance. Follow the exact product, infill, installer, and warranty instructions; frequency depends on trees, shade, traffic, pets, weather, and drainage.

  • Remove leaves, litter, spills, and pet waste promptly.
  • Keep drains and flow paths clear; observe ponding after rain.
  • Brush, decompact, replenish infill, or rinse only with approved tools and methods.
  • Inspect seams, edges, transitions, wear, weeds, and seasonal movement, especially after storms and freeze-thaw cycles.

Turf needs no mowing or routine irrigation, but cleaning and temporary cooling can use water, and repairs or weed control may still be needed. See the four-season care guide.

Act promptly: lift solids and blot liquids without spreading them, then contain the residue. Use only tools and cleaners approved in writing for the exact fibres, backing, adhesive, and infill, following the product directions.

Do not use mineral spirits, paint remover, degreaser, a metal scraper, or another strong treatment unless the manufacturer approves it for that system. Do not wash oil, paint, solvent, or contaminated rinse water into soil or a storm drain. Ask the manufacturer or installer how to handle an unknown or stubborn stain and follow Peel Region disposal guidance where applicable.

Drainage is a complete site-system question, not just a carpet flow-rate claim. Performance depends on the turf backing, infill, base storage, native soil, grading, outlets, freeze-thaw conditions, rainfall, and clear drains or swales.

The design must preserve a safe path away from buildings and neighbouring properties and include overflow for heavy rain. Artificial turf alone cannot guarantee that a yard will not flood. Mississauga says property drainage is the landowner's responsibility; review the City's property-drainage guidance.

Municipal Rules & Bylaws

Check current rules for each address

Toronto states that artificial turf is not soft landscaping and does not count toward required soft-landscaping area.

Front- and rear-yard requirements vary by lot frontage, zoning, and the property. Review the City's artificial-turf guidance and residential landscaping rules.

Confirm the current requirements for the address with Toronto Building before work begins.

Mississauga: Do not assume blanket approval or count turf toward a required landscaped area without written confirmation. Zoning, the driveway, grading, drainage, approved swales, and site-specific provisions can affect a project. The City's multi-residential/non-residential stormwater-credit policy classifies artificial turf as impervious area; that policy does not by itself decide zoning for a single-family home. Start with the City's zoning information and request address-specific confirmation.

Brampton: The City's current zoning definition excludes artificial turf from “permeable landscaping, residential” in its driveway rules. This is not a blanket ban on every residential turf project. Review the current driveway guidance and confirm the proposed scope with the City.

Vaughan: City materials currently conflict. The Building Standards FAQ says artificial turf is not soft landscaping, while the City-posted base text of Zoning By-law 001-2021 includes artificial turf in soft landscaping when it is permeable. Review the official Vaughan FAQ and posted zoning by-law (PDF). Do not count turf toward a required soft-landscaping area without written, address-specific confirmation from Vaughan Zoning.

Richmond Hill: By-law 93-25 defines synthetic turf as hard landscaping, but the City says the by-law is currently under Ontario Land Tribunal appeal and that former zoning by-laws may still apply. Review the current definitions (PDF) and appeal status, then obtain property-specific confirmation from the City.

A building permit may not be the relevant approval, but that does not make a project automatically approved. Zoning, grading or site-alteration approval may apply, as may requirements for retaining walls, driveways, curb or boulevard work, protected trees, conservation-regulated land, condominium property, easements, or utilities.

Before excavation, the excavator must submit a locate request and wait for the required responses; see Ontario One Call. Confirm property boundaries and obtain written direction from the municipality and any other authority that applies to the address. This is general information, not legal advice or municipal approval.

Health & Safety Questions

Evidence, product documentation, and practical precautions

The current written proposal should name the exact infill; do not infer it from a package label or a different project. If no infill or a non-rubber infill is proposed, that should also be explicit.

Current government assessments of recycled tire crumb on sports fields are generally reassuring for the human chemical exposures they studied, but they do not settle every residential, pet, heat, runoff, or ecological question. Review the exact specification and intended use rather than labelling every crumb-rubber system “toxic” or “safe.” See the 2026 OEHHA assessment and the 2025 Canadian evidence review.

No. We do not publish a blanket PFAS-free guarantee without current evidence for every component of the exact proposed system. “PFAS-free” is ambiguous unless its scope is defined.

Ask whether the evidence is a “no intentionally added PFAS” declaration or laboratory testing; which fibres, backing, coating, infill, and pad it covers; the method, analyte scope, reporting limits, laboratory, sample identity, and date. California DTSC says it is still verifying reported market removal of PFAS-based ingredients from artificial turf. Read the DTSC project update.

Yes, it can. Artificial turf can become much hotter than living grass in direct sun, and hot ground can burn paw pads or contribute to heat illness. There is no universal product or air-temperature cutoff that guarantees safe use.

Check actual surface conditions, provide shade and fresh water, and use cooler times of day; keep the dog off when the surface is hot. Water may cool turf only briefly. Watch for paw injury or heat-stress signs and contact a veterinarian if concerned. See the Ontario SPCA hot-surface guidance.

Durability & Warranty

Service life, winter, warranties, and repairs

Service life varies. Relevant factors include:

  • The exact product and complete system
  • Amount of foot traffic
  • UV exposure
  • Installation, drainage, and site conditions
  • Maintenance, contamination, heat, and accidental damage

There is no evidence-backed universal number of years for every residential installation. Review the manufacturer's current product documentation for the exact SKU and remember that an estimated service life is not the same as a warranty term.

A GTA installation should account for:

  • Freeze-thaw movement and base preparation
  • Grading, drainage, snow, and ice
  • The selected product's temperature limitations
  • Care methods that do not damage fibres or edges

Performance is site- and system-specific, so these factors should be addressed in the proposal rather than guaranteed generally.

Manufacturer product coverage and YardGuard workmanship terms are separate. The exact duration, defects, exclusions, maintenance duties, remedies, and claims process are governed by the written documents supplied for the selected product and contracted installation. See our warranty information page for the documentation process.

Written warranty terms do not replace statutory consumer rights. Ontario's Consumer Protection Act deems services supplied under a consumer agreement to be of reasonably acceptable quality; review the Province's renovation guidance or obtain legal advice for a specific dispute.

Sometimes. Repairability depends on the damage, backing, seams, base, product availability, batch, pile direction, colour change, and location. Possible approaches include:

  • Patching a localized area where compatible turf is available
  • Replacing a larger section when a patch is unsuitable
  • Re-seaming or re-securing an edge after the cause is corrected

A repair may remain visible and cannot be promised as seamless, quick, or inexpensive before inspection. For melted turf, identify and correct the heat source first; reflected sunlight, hot equipment, flame, or exhaust can otherwise damage it again.
